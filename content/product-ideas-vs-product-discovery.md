# Product ideas are not product discovery

AI makes it very cheap to generate product ideas.

That is useful. It can widen the search space, help teams imagine new workflows, expose technical possibilities, and make brainstorming much faster.

The management mistake is treating this as automatic:

> **We have a list of plausible products, therefore we have identified products worth building.**

A product idea is a hypothesis. Product discovery is the work required to find out whether that hypothesis deserves investment.

## Ideation is useful — but it is only one stage

A healthy sequence is:

**Idea → problem hypothesis → user/customer evidence → workflow hypothesis → prototype → technical feasibility → delivery feasibility → commercial evidence → product decision**

Teams do not have to execute this as a rigid waterfall. In practice, several steps overlap and feed back into one another.

The important distinction is that **idea generation and opportunity validation are different achievements**.

A brainstorm can produce ten ideas in an hour. That does not mean ten customer opportunities have been discovered.

## Engineers can originate product ideas

This guide is not arguing that product thinking belongs only to product managers, sales teams, or executives.

Developers, engineers, architects, data specialists, researchers, designers, support teams, and domain experts often see opportunities that others miss because they understand constraints, systems, failure modes, new technical capabilities, or repetitive work in unusually concrete ways.

An engineer may legitimately identify:

- a workflow that could be automated;
- a new capability enabled by a model or tool;
- a recurring integration problem;
- an expensive operational bottleneck;
- a product opportunity created by a new technical primitive;
- an internal capability that could become a customer-facing service.

That can be excellent product input.

The distinction is:

> **Originating the idea does not exempt the idea from discovery.**

## Product names are not product definitions

A name can make an idea feel much more concrete than it really is.

For example, a team might invent labels such as:

- AI Operations Assistant;
- Smart Compliance Copilot;
- Automated Design Reviewer;
- Intelligent Asset Planner;
- AI Project Coordinator.

Those names may be useful placeholders. But each can hide dozens of unanswered questions:

- Who is the user?
- Who is the buyer?
- What problem are they trying to solve?
- How do they solve it today?
- How frequently does the problem occur?
- What does the current problem cost in time, money, risk, or lost opportunity?
- What part of the workflow would actually change?
- What output or decision must be good enough for the product to be useful?
- What systems, data, permissions, or integrations are required?
- What happens when the AI is wrong?
- Who reviews, approves, or acts on the output?
- How will the product be supported and maintained?
- Why would the customer adopt this rather than continue with the current process?
- Why would they pay this supplier rather than use a general-purpose AI tool directly?

Naming something does not answer these questions.

## AI creates idea abundance

Generative AI changes product ideation because the cost of producing plausible concepts is now extremely low.

A team can ask an AI system to produce:

- twenty product names;
- ten customer personas;
- fifty possible workflows;
- mock landing pages;
- feature lists;
- pricing tiers;
- screenshots;
- demos;
- market-positioning copy.

The result can look like a portfolio strategy before much external evidence exists.

This creates a useful discipline:

> **When ideas become cheap, evidence becomes more important.**

The scarce resource is no longer necessarily the ability to imagine a product. It may be the ability to distinguish a valuable opportunity from a plausible-looking one.

## Separate three kinds of evidence

### 1. Problem evidence

Evidence that a meaningful problem exists.

Examples:

- user interviews;
- observed workarounds;
- support requests;
- repeated manual processes;
- error or delay data;
- procurement requests;
- existing spending;
- compliance or operational pain;
- lost revenue or avoidable cost.

### 2. Solution evidence

Evidence that the proposed product or workflow actually helps.

Examples:

- prototype testing;
- task completion;
- quality measures;
- technical feasibility;
- integration tests;
- acceptance criteria;
- reliability and failure handling;
- user willingness to change behaviour.

### 3. Business evidence

Evidence that providing the solution is economically and operationally worthwhile.

Examples:

- willingness and ability to pay;
- procurement feasibility;
- cost to deliver and support;
- expected margin or avoided cost;
- implementation effort;
- sales cycle;
- switching cost;
- retention potential;
- legal, security, or regulatory burden;
- strategic fit.

A product idea can be technically exciting while still lacking one or more of these evidence types.

## Product discovery is cross-functional

No single function has all of the information needed to validate a product opportunity.

| Perspective | What it contributes |
| --- | --- |
| **Engineering / technical** | Feasibility, architecture, technical constraints, integration, cost drivers, failure modes, new capabilities. |
| **Domain experts** | Correctness, workflow context, professional standards, exceptions, semantics, real-world operating constraints. |
| **Product / user research / design** | User needs, task context, usability, adoption, problem framing, prototype evidence. |
| **Commercial / sales** | Buyer needs, procurement, competition, pricing, sales cycle, willingness to pay, channel. |
| **Operations / support** | Deployment, support burden, maintainability, incident handling, service quality. |
| **Security / privacy / legal / compliance** | Constraints, risk, obligations, acceptable use, contractual boundaries where relevant. |
| **Finance / leadership** | Investment threshold, strategic fit, portfolio trade-offs, expected return. |

Not every small experiment needs all of these roles formally involved.

The principle is that **the evidence needed to make the decision should be represented somewhere**.

## Do not outsource product strategy to brainstorming

A company can ask every team member to contribute product ideas. That can be a healthy way to surface opportunities.

But the exercise should be labelled correctly.

It is:

- an ideation exercise;
- a hypothesis-generation exercise;
- a technology-scouting exercise;
- a way to collect frontline observations.

It is not automatically:

- customer discovery;
- market validation;
- product strategy;
- a committed roadmap;
- evidence that a product should exist.

The dangerous transition is:

**“Please suggest ideas” → “These are now our products.”**

The missing work is discovery and prioritisation.

## A better way to ask technical teams for product ideas

Instead of asking only for a product name, ask for a short opportunity hypothesis.

For each proposed idea, require:

1. **User / customer** — who has the problem?
2. **Problem** — what are they trying to do, and what is difficult today?
3. **Current workaround** — how is the problem handled now?
4. **Evidence** — what makes us believe this problem is real?
5. **Desired outcome** — what should improve if we solve it?
6. **Technical opportunity** — what new capability makes a better solution possible?
7. **Unknowns** — what do we still need to learn?
8. **Cheapest test** — what experiment would reduce uncertainty fastest?

This keeps engineers involved in innovation without pretending that ideation itself completes product discovery.

## A useful product-opportunity template

> **We believe [user/customer] has difficulty with [problem] because [evidence].**
>
> **Today they use [current process/workaround], which creates [cost, delay, risk, or limitation].**
>
> **We think [technical/product capability] could improve [measurable outcome].**
>
> **The largest unknowns are [assumptions].**
>
> **The cheapest next test is [experiment].**

The point is not paperwork. The point is to make assumptions visible before an attractive name or prototype hardens into a roadmap commitment.

## AI can help with discovery — but should not impersonate customers

AI can be useful for:

- summarising existing research;
- clustering interview notes;
- generating alternative hypotheses;
- drafting interview guides;
- creating prototype variants;
- analysing support logs;
- mapping workflows;
- identifying assumptions that need testing;
- preparing experiments.

But synthetic personas and generated market narratives should not silently replace evidence from real customers, users, buyers, operations, or domain experts.

A generated explanation of why a hypothetical customer would buy something is still a hypothesis unless grounded in evidence.

## One important asymmetry

AI makes it easier to generate both **ideas** and **solutions**.

That can create a strange situation:

> The team can build a convincing prototype before it has strong evidence that the problem deserves solving.

This connects directly to the project's broader concepts:

- **completion illusion** — the prototype can look finished before the product question is settled;
- **substance gap** — the visible artefact can contain more plausible assumptions than verified customer-specific substance;
- **capability-to-product leap** — technical capability is mistaken for a product opportunity;
- **value gap** — a working product is mistaken for a worthwhile business outcome.

## Ten questions before an idea becomes a roadmap item

1. **Who specifically has the problem?**
2. **What are they trying to accomplish?**
3. **How do they handle it today?**
4. **What evidence shows the current approach is painful or costly enough to change?**
5. **What outcome would improve if the idea works?**
6. **Which part of the proposed solution is genuinely differentiated or newly possible?**
7. **What must be true technically for the product to work?**
8. **What must be true commercially or operationally for the product to be viable?**
9. **What are the biggest assumptions or reasons the idea could fail?**
10. **What is the cheapest experiment that would reduce the most important uncertainty?**

## Public guidance supporting the distinction

The GOV.UK Service Standard says teams should develop a deep understanding of users and the problem they are trying to solve, and explicitly warns that focusing on a preselected solution can cause teams to miss the real problem. It recommends testing assumptions early and using prototypes to test hypotheses.

The GOV.UK Service Manual also states that opinions or suggestions that do not come from users should be treated as assumptions to be tested through research, and that multidisciplinary teams should combine product, user research, design, development, delivery, and other skills.

The UK Government AI Playbook similarly recommends that AI use cases be led by business and user needs, pain points, and inefficiencies rather than by technology capability alone.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework prioritises interdisciplinary participation, defining business value or business use, intended tasks, system requirements, expected benefits, and expected costs in context.

These sources do not establish one universal product-development process. They support the narrower principle that **ideas and technical capabilities should be tested against real user, workflow, organisational, and business context before being treated as validated products**.

## One sentence for a management meeting

> **Generate product ideas widely. Validate product opportunities rigorously.**
