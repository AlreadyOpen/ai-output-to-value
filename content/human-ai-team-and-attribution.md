# Treat human + AI as a team, not an academic paper

A great deal of discussion about AI attribution starts from the wrong model.

Whenever AI contributes to a piece of work, people quickly ask who the author is, whether the AI should be acknowledged, how much was done by the human, and whether every model or tool involved should be disclosed.

Those questions make sense in particular contexts, especially academic research. Academic authorship and acknowledgement have specific purposes: identifying intellectual contribution, documenting methods, disclosing support, and making research open to scrutiny and reproduction.

But most human activity is not an academic paper.

Normal business, engineering, design, construction, hospitality, manufacturing, administration, software delivery, and operations do not attach a complete contributor statement to every output.

A better model for much ordinary work may therefore be:

> **Treat the human and their AI systems as a team — or, at a larger scale, as an organisation.**

This is not as historically novel as it first appears. Business has been absorbing work into machines, software, and organisational systems for decades.

## Computerisation already moved work into machines

The history matters because AI is not the first technology to make part of the production process less visible.

IBM introduced the 1401 Data Processing System in 1959. IBM's own history describes it as a system that brought computing into everyday business functions including accounting, inventory control, billing, and payroll. It specifically notes that payroll processing could be automated through to printing the cheques.

That was already a substantial transfer of clerical work from people into a machine.

Yet business did not develop a convention that every payslip must publicly acknowledge the computer model that calculated it.

The employer remained responsible for paying employees correctly. The computer became part of the employer's operating system.

This distinction is still useful:

**important operational involvement does not automatically imply public authorship.**

## The spreadsheet repeated the pattern

The same transition happened when personal computers entered ordinary office work.

The Computer History Museum describes VisiCalc, developed in 1979, as the program that helped turn the personal computer into a business machine. One of its central capabilities was automated spreadsheet recalculation, allowing users to change an input and immediately explore the consequences.

Before electronic spreadsheets, people could spend substantial time manually recalculating models.

Afterwards, software performed much of that mechanical calculation.

Eventually this became so ordinary that nobody expected a financial model to include an acknowledgement such as:

> "The analyst acknowledges the spreadsheet application for recalculating cells F12 through H37."

Nor do we normally say that an analyst did not really perform an analysis because a computer executed thousands of arithmetic operations.

Instead, we ask whether the assumptions were sensible, whether formulas were correct, whether data was reliable, and who stands behind the conclusion.

AI creates harder cases, but this is an important historical baseline.

## Engineering already moved from manual drafting to digital systems

Engineering made a similar transition.

NIST described the development of CAD/CAM from two-dimensional wireframe drafting systems in the 1970s toward parametric and feature-based solid modelling in the 1990s.

Modern engineering work can involve CAD, BIM, finite-element analysis, CFD, meshing, optimisation, clash detection, automated quantity extraction, rendering, digital fabrication, and many other computational processes.

These systems can perform work that would previously have required very large amounts of human effort.

But the normal public identity of a drawing or report is still the responsible engineer, consultant, contractor, or company.

We do not normally put every CAD vendor, GPU vendor, operating system, numerical library, printer driver, and workstation model into the author field merely because those technologies contributed to production.

Their exact identities can matter greatly for debugging, validation, reproducibility, procurement, safety, or an investigation.

That is **technical provenance**.

It is not necessarily **public authorship**.

## Digitalisation has been hiding complexity for decades

The same pattern continued through databases, email, enterprise software, search engines, cloud computing, ERP systems, automated manufacturing, reservation systems, digital control systems, logistics platforms, and countless other technologies.

The recipient of the final service increasingly sees less of the machinery behind it.

When a bank processes a transaction, a customer normally does not know every database, operating system, server, network appliance, software service, and employee involved.

When an airline issues a boarding pass, a passenger does not receive the contributor list for the reservation, ticketing, pricing, scheduling, payment, identity, and airport systems behind it.

When an engineer sends a PDF, the client may have no idea how many software systems transformed the information before that PDF appeared.

Digitalisation did not make organisational responsibility disappear.

It made the organisation itself a more important abstraction layer.

## We already attribute complex work to organisations

Consider large organisations such as TSMC, NVIDIA, Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, BlackRock, McDonald's, or KFC.

Most people recognise the organisation.

Almost nobody outside it knows every person working inside it.

A customer buying a smartphone does not know every engineer, manufacturing specialist, software developer, procurement employee, logistics planner, designer, lawyer, accountant, security specialist, retail worker, cleaner, contractor, and supplier employee involved in bringing that product to market.

Someone buying an NVIDIA GPU does not know every engineer who contributed to architecture, firmware, drivers, validation, packaging, manufacturing, documentation, developer tools, operations, and support.

Yet it remains perfectly meaningful to say:

> **"This is an NVIDIA product."**

The organisation is the public abstraction for a very large coordinated system.

That abstraction does not erase the individual workers inside it. It makes large-scale coordinated activity possible to describe and govern.

The same principle can apply when some of the participating systems are AI.

## Knowing the boss does not mean knowing the organisation

Sometimes the public knows the CEO, founder, chair, or another prominent executive.

That does not mean the leader personally knows every employee.

It also does not mean the leader knows every server, workstation, software package, library, manufacturing machine, supplier, contractor, internal service, database, or tool being used throughout the organisation.

At sufficient scale, that would be unrealistic.

This is precisely why organisations use layers of responsibility:

- boards and executives govern at one level;
- business-unit leaders govern another;
- managers own teams and processes;
- specialists own technical domains;
- operators own particular procedures;
- automated systems perform other parts of the workflow.

A CEO can retire or be replaced and the company continues.

Employees join and leave. Software changes. Factories are upgraded. Suppliers change. Machines are replaced.

The organisation persists.

That reveals something important:

> **The organisation is not simply an extension of the person at the top.**

It is a system of people, processes, delegated authority, records, infrastructure, machines, software, and institutional knowledge.

More importantly:

> **The organisation knows more collectively than any one participant knows individually.**

That was true before AI.

## The restaurant shows both sides of the argument

A restaurant makes the point particularly clearly.

You may eat at a neighbourhood restaurant, a luxury hotel, a famous fine-dining restaurant, or even a three-Michelin-star restaurant.

You normally know the restaurant's name.

You may know the executive chef.

But you probably do not know every sous-chef, chef de partie, pastry chef, prep cook, dishwasher, waiter, sommelier, host, cashier, cleaner, purchaser, maintenance worker, and manager who contributed to the experience.

That does not mean their contribution is unimportant.

It means the **restaurant is the operational unit presented to the customer**.

And now consider the tools.

You also probably do not know:

- the brands and models of the ovens;
- how many induction units are installed;
- which refrigerator stored particular ingredients;
- which mixer or food processor was used;
- which dishwasher cleaned the plates;
- which thermometers, vacuum sealers, or sous-vide systems were involved;
- which POS and reservation systems coordinated the service;
- or how many of each appliance the kitchen owns.

Even in a very expensive restaurant, the diner is not normally handed a complete equipment inventory with the menu.

We do not expect:

> "Prepared by twelve named kitchen employees with assistance from two ovens, three food processors, four induction units, a specialist mixer, a refrigeration system, and a commercial dishwasher."

The people matter.

The tools matter.

The records may matter.

But the public attribution normally remains the restaurant.

## If AI is a team member, we do not list every team member

Suppose we treat an AI agent as something closer to a team member because it can analyse information, propose alternatives, use tools, produce substantial work, coordinate tasks, review outputs, and continue a workflow.

Even then, universal public acknowledgement does not automatically follow.

Ordinary organisations do not name every human team member on every output.

A consulting report may carry the firm's name.

A software product may carry the company's name.

A building may be identified with an architect, engineer, contractor, or developer without listing every person who contributed to every intermediate artefact.

The organisation coordinates the contributors and accepts responsibility for the result.

A human-AI organisation can use the same abstraction.

## If AI is only a tool, we do not list every tool either

Now take the opposite position.

Suppose AI is not a team member at all. Suppose it is merely a tool.

The same attribution conclusion still does not follow.

We already do not name every tool used in ordinary work.

An engineer may use a CAD system, spreadsheet, compiler, database, search engine, simulation package, operating system, GPU, cloud service, and many software libraries while producing one deliverable.

A restaurant may use dozens or hundreds of appliances and systems.

A manufacturing company may use thousands of machines, software components, and automated controls.

The mere fact that a tool materially contributed to production does not mean it belongs in the public author list.

So the argument has two independent sides:

> **If AI is a collaborator, we already do not list every collaborator. If AI is a tool, we already do not list every tool.**

The remaining question is not whether AI participated.

The remaining question is what kind of disclosure, traceability, control, and responsibility the context actually requires.

## AI is still different from a conventional appliance

The historical analogy should not be pushed too far.

An oven does not normally decide what the restaurant should cook.

A traditional spreadsheet executes formulas supplied by a person.

Traditional CAD usually acts within commands and constraints specified by users.

Modern AI systems can do more. They may generate text or software, synthesise information, search, compare alternatives, select tools, critique another system's output, communicate with customers, and execute multi-step tasks.

The repo's separate analysis in [AI can do business work too](ai-business-capability-and-judgement.md) treats analysis, judgement, authority, and accountability as distinct questions for exactly this reason.

AI therefore creates new governance problems.

But **stronger governance is not the same thing as universal public coauthorship**.

## Attribution, provenance, and accountability are different

Three concepts are often collapsed into one.

**Attribution** asks whose name appears on the work.

**Provenance** asks how the work was produced.

**Accountability** asks where responsibility and recourse sit.

They can overlap, but they are not identical.

A restaurant can maintain staffing records, supplier records, maintenance logs, temperature logs, and appliance inventories without printing all of them on the menu.

An engineering consultancy can retain model versions, calculation histories, approvals, software versions, test results, and design-change records without printing all of them on the title block.

A human-AI organisation can similarly retain detailed records such as:

- which models were called;
- which agents performed which roles;
- what tools they used;
- what data they accessed;
- what intermediate outputs they produced;
- which automated checks ran;
- where escalation occurred;
- which human or authorised system approved an action;
- and which final artefact resulted.

Those records may be essential for assurance.

But it does not follow that all of them need to be displayed on every customer-facing output.

> **Internal traceability, individual knowledge, and external attribution are three different things.**

## Responsibility does not require omniscience

A common response to AI delegation is to insist that a human must know everything the AI did.

That standard is not how large organisations already work.

A CEO does not personally inspect every transaction.

A project director does not personally perform every calculation.

A principal engineer does not personally write every line of software.

A restaurant owner does not personally watch every plate being prepared.

Responsibility in complex organisations is created through structured delegation, controls, review, testing, records, escalation, and authority boundaries.

A human-AI organisation can be designed the same way.

For example:

**AI proposes → automated checks run → specialists review where required → authorised actors approve → the organisation accepts responsibility.**

Different work needs different controls.

A marketing image, routine code refactoring, a financial transaction, a medical decision, and a structural engineering calculation should not be treated as equivalent.

But the presence of AI does not make organisational responsibility impossible.

It makes governance more important.

## Disclosure should be contextual, not ritual

None of this argues that AI should always be hidden.

There are many situations where disclosure is important or mandatory:

- academic research and methodological reporting;
- regulated professional work;
- safety-critical systems;
- contractual requirements;
- privacy and confidentiality restrictions;
- client rules about permitted models or data locations;
- legal proceedings or audits;
- cases where a particular person is explicitly represented as having personally performed the work.

The point is narrower.

There is a major difference between:

> **"Maintain an auditable record of AI involvement where it affects accountability."**

and:

> **"Every output involving AI must publicly acknowledge every model and agent that contributed to it."**

The first is a governance requirement.

The second imports an academic-style attribution convention into contexts where it may have little operational meaning.

## The historical pattern matters

Seen historically, generative AI is not an isolated break from everything that came before.

Business computing moved accounting and payroll into machines.

Electronic spreadsheets moved repeated recalculation into software.

Word processing moved large parts of document production into software.

CAD and engineering software moved drafting, modelling, and calculation into digital systems.

Databases moved organisational memory into software.

Enterprise systems automated large parts of administration.

Cloud computing made even the physical machines increasingly invisible to many users.

AI now moves more analysis, generation, coordination, evaluation, and action into software.

Each transition changes what humans do.

But historically we did not respond by turning every ordinary business output into an academic contributor statement.

We changed workflows, controls, professions, organisations, and our understanding of responsibility.

AI may require that process again.

## From digital organisation to human-AI organisation

The more useful transition may therefore not be:

**human authorship → AI authorship**

but:

**human organisation → digital organisation → human-AI organisation**

A future company may contain:

- human employees;
- conventional software;
- specialised AI models;
- autonomous or semi-autonomous agents;
- deterministic control systems;
- robots and other machines;
- external suppliers and contractors.

Some people may direct AI agents.

Some agents may coordinate other agents.

Humans may intervene only where particular authority, expertise, relationship, or judgement is required.

No single person — and no single AI — will understand the entire organisation.

That is not fundamentally alien.

We already built organisations too complicated for any individual to understand completely.

AI adds a new kind of participant to them.

## The more useful questions

The future question should not always be:

**"Did AI help create this?"**

Increasingly, the answer will often be yes.

Nor is it enough to ask:

**"Which AI should receive credit?"**

The more useful questions are:

- Who or what stands behind the result?
- Who has authority to approve important decisions?
- How is responsibility delegated?
- Can consequential decisions be traced when necessary?
- Can errors be investigated and corrected?
- Are appropriate controls applied to the actual risk?
- Does the customer, regulator, auditor, or public need to know something specific about the production process?
- Is the organisation capable of supporting and taking responsibility for the result?

Those questions scale much better than trying to turn every ordinary activity into an academic paper with an acknowledgement section.

We did not list the mainframe on every payslip.

We do not credit the spreadsheet engine on every financial model.

We do not list every CAD component on an engineering drawing.

We do not list every kitchen worker or every appliance on a restaurant menu.

We do not know every employee or every tool inside a large company.

And knowing the CEO does not change that.

For decades, computers and digitalisation have progressively moved parts of human work into technological systems.

AI continues that history while pushing automation into activities that previously looked more distinctly human.

The challenge is therefore not simply deciding whether an AI deserves a byline.

It is learning how to build **accountable human-AI organisations**, where responsibility and important provenance remain clear even though no customer — and no single participant inside the organisation — needs to know every human, every AI, and every tool involved.
