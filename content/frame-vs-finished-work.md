# A strong frame is not the same as a finished job

AI makes it possible to move from an idea to a polished, coherent artefact very quickly. A good brief, information architecture, visual direction, page structure, component system, or generated application scaffold can be genuinely valuable work.

The management mistake is to treat that progress as evidence that everything remaining is merely technical execution.

> **A frame can define the shape of the answer without establishing that the answer is complete.**

This matters especially in client-facing work because generative tools are very good at filling gaps with plausible defaults. The result can therefore look much more resolved than the underlying decisions actually are.

## What a strong frame can legitimately establish

Depending on the job, a strong frame may establish:

- the intended audience;
- the positioning or central message;
- the broad information architecture;
- visual direction and tone;
- major sections, screens, or workflows;
- an initial component hierarchy;
- a demonstration of the desired interaction pattern;
- constraints that should guide later work;
- an initial prioritisation of features or messages.

These are substantive contributions. They should not be dismissed merely because AI helped create them.

But they are not the whole job.

## The completion illusion

A traditional early-stage project often looked unfinished because the missing work was visible: blank pages, incomplete screens, missing copy, disconnected systems, or placeholder functionality.

Generative systems can fill those spaces immediately. They can produce polished copy, realistic interfaces, routes, forms, dashboards, data structures, animations, and working demonstrations before the organisation has resolved every requirement behind them.

This creates a **completion illusion**:

> **The artefact looks resolved, so the unresolved work becomes harder to see.**

The problem is not the polish. The problem is using polish as a proxy for completion.

## “The frame is done; only technical work remains” is often too coarse

After a frame or scaffold exists, several different kinds of work may remain. Some are technical. Others are product, editorial, operational, commercial, legal, or organisational.

| Layer | Typical questions still to resolve |
| --- | --- |
| **Direction** | Who is this for? What outcome are we trying to create? What should the user understand or do? |
| **Content substance** | Are the claims accurate? Is the language specific to the organisation? Is important evidence missing? What must not be said publicly? |
| **Product / workflow** | What actually happens when a user submits, signs in, searches, pays, uploads, approves, or requests something? |
| **Interaction & UX** | Does the flow work for real users, devices, accessibility needs, errors, empty states, slow networks, and edge cases? |
| **Technical implementation** | How are data, identity, integrations, APIs, infrastructure, deployment, performance, and security implemented? |
| **Acceptance & assurance** | What needs to be tested or reviewed before the intended audience can rely on it? |
| **Operations** | Who receives alerts or enquiries? Who owns content updates, backups, incidents, analytics, support, and maintenance? |
| **Commercial delivery** | What exactly has been promised? What is in scope? Who can approve changes? What happens after launch? |

A project may have most of its visual frame complete while several of these layers remain open.

## A frame does not determine how much work remains

There is no reliable percentage such as:

> “The pages are already there, so the job is 80% complete.”

Completion percentage depends on the acceptance criteria, not on how much of the screen is filled.

For one job, the interface may genuinely represent most of the work. For another, the visible interface may be a small part of the total effort because the difficult work is integration, migration, security, domain logic, content verification, operations, or support.

The correct question is therefore not:

> **How complete does it look?**

It is:

> **Which acceptance criteria have actually been satisfied, and which remain open?**

## Separate five useful claims

A team should be able to say which of these claims it is making:

1. **We have a direction.**  
   The intended positioning, structure, or experience is clear enough to guide the next stage.

2. **We have a convincing prototype.**  
   The artefact demonstrates the intended experience well enough for discussion, testing, or decision-making.

3. **We have implemented the required system.**  
   The required functionality and integrations exist in the intended environment.

4. **We have an accepted deliverable.**  
   The result satisfies agreed criteria for the intended client or user.

5. **We have an operable service.**  
   Ownership, maintenance, monitoring, support, change, security, and continuity are defined well enough for ongoing use.

Evidence for claim 1 is not automatically evidence for claim 5.

## The most useful handoff is not “make this technical”

A stronger handoff identifies what has already been decided and what is still open.

For example:

### Decisions already made

- target audience;
- desired positioning;
- visual direction;
- high-level navigation;
- priority messages;
- known constraints.

### Decisions still requiring work

- exact functional behaviour;
- authoritative content and claims;
- real data and integrations;
- identity and permissions;
- error and edge-case behaviour;
- security and privacy controls;
- accessibility and performance targets;
- form or workflow destinations;
- deployment and environment ownership;
- analytics and measurement;
- acceptance criteria;
- post-launch support and maintenance.

This turns the frame into useful input rather than treating it as proof that the remaining job is commodity implementation.

## The professional value may be in making the invisible work visible

When AI can cheaply generate the visible artefact, professional value shifts toward things that are harder to infer from appearance:

- deciding what problem should be solved;
- distinguishing requirements from plausible defaults;
- applying domain knowledge;
- validating claims and assumptions;
- choosing appropriate trade-offs;
- integrating with the client’s actual environment;
- making risk proportionate to consequence;
- defining acceptance criteria;
- operating and supporting the result;
- taking responsibility for what is delivered.

AI can assist with all of these too. The distinction is not **human work versus AI work**. It is **established work versus assumed work**.

## A management test

When presented with a polished AI-generated frame or scaffold, ask:

1. **Which decisions are actually settled by this artefact?**
2. **Which parts are demonstrations or plausible defaults rather than confirmed requirements?**
3. **What nontechnical work remains, not just what code remains?**
4. **What would have to be true before we could call the result accepted by a client?**
5. **Who will operate, support, correct, and update it after delivery?**

The purpose of these questions is not to devalue a strong frame. It is to value it accurately.

## One sentence for a management meeting

> **A strong frame can remove a great deal of uncertainty, but it does not turn every unresolved product, content, operational, and assurance decision into “just technical work.”**
