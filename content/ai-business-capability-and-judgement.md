# AI can do business work too: judgement, authority, and accountability are different

AI capability is not confined to coding, drafting, or back-office automation.

Modern AI systems can participate in activities organisations traditionally describe as business work:

- analysing reports, files, tables, and other information;
- preparing proposals, pitches, presentations, scripts, briefs, and customer communications;
- answering enquiries and explaining products;
- researching alternatives and comparing options;
- preparing recommendations and decision material;
- conducting live voice interactions;
- adapting messages to questions or concerns;
- using tools and APIs to continue a workflow;
- producing or coordinating multimodal content;
- monitoring and acting within delegated limits.

The project should therefore avoid a misleading division in which AI is treated as capable of implementation while **judgement, persuasion, analysis, strategy, or customer interaction are assumed to belong uniquely to humans**.

> **Judgement is a capability to evaluate. Authority is permission to act. Accountability is where responsibility and recourse sit. They are related, but they are not the same thing.**

## 1. Business work is not defined by being human

A proposal does not become strategically insightful because a human wrote it. A presentation does not become persuasive because a senior executive delivered it. A customer reply does not become helpful because a salesperson typed it manually.

Likewise, an AI-generated proposal, analysis, presentation, or customer interaction is not automatically good because the model is advanced.

The useful questions are actor-neutral:

- Is the content accurate enough for the intended use?
- Does it address the real customer problem?
- Does the interaction improve the desired outcome?
- Are assumptions and uncertainty visible?
- Is the actor operating within the right authority?
- Can failures be detected and recovered from?
- Is the result worth the total cost and risk?

## 2. AI can participate in persuasion and customer interaction

Persuasion is not a uniquely human capability.

A 2025 *Nature Human Behaviour* study comparing GPT-4 and humans in structured debates found no significant difference between non-personalised AI and the human baseline, while personalised GPT-4 was more persuasive than the human baseline in that experimental condition. A later 2025 meta-analysis found **no significant overall difference** between LLMs and humans across the small, heterogeneous collection of eligible persuasion studies.

That evidence does **not** establish that an AI system is automatically a good salesperson, negotiator, consultant, public speaker, or account manager. Those tasks include product knowledge, context, timing, trust, compliance, pricing authority, follow-up, and failure handling.

It does establish a narrower point:

> **Persuasion, explanation, objection handling, and adaptive argument should not be treated as permanent human monopolies.**

The same discipline applies to customer support. Research on a generative-AI conversational assistant used by 5,179 customer-support agents found a 14% average increase in issues resolved per hour in that specific setting, with larger gains among novice and lower-skilled workers. That was an **AI-assisted human workflow**, not evidence that autonomous AI support always outperforms people.

## 3. Realtime AI changes the interface, not the standard of proof

Current realtime AI APIs can process and produce live audio and text, maintain conversational sessions, and use tools. This makes live AI interaction technically practical through web applications, voice systems, telephony, kiosks, and other interfaces.

Combined with an appropriate embodied system, a conversational layer could also be connected to a robot or other physical interface.

But keep the claims separate:

**Realtime speech capability** ≠ **effective selling, presenting, advising, or customer service**.

A customer-facing AI or embodied agent can be evaluated on:

- factual accuracy;
- successful resolution;
- customer satisfaction;
- legitimate conversion or progression measures;
- correct objection handling;
- latency and conversational quality;
- accessibility and language coverage;
- safe tool use;
- disclosure and consent where required;
- escalation and recovery;
- compliance with policy and law;
- total operating cost.

A convincing voice or physical presence is a channel. It is not proof of business effectiveness.

## 4. AI can analyse company information rapidly, but analysis still needs an evaluation target

Modern model APIs can accept files and images and use tools such as retrieval/search, code execution, and custom functions.

That enables workflows such as:

- comparing company reports;
- extracting and reconciling facts across documents;
- calculating metrics from supplied data;
- generating charts and summaries;
- identifying anomalies or missing information;
- preparing questions for management;
- comparing scenarios;
- drafting recommendations;
- connecting analysis to company systems through tools.

The mistake can go in either direction:

> **“AI analysed it, therefore it must be correct.”**

or

> **“Only a human analyst can exercise judgement.”**

Both are shortcuts. The useful standard is task-specific performance: what evidence shows the analysis is accurate, complete enough, calibrated, useful, and robust to the failure modes that matter?

## 5. Human judgement is not a correctness certificate

Human decision-makers can make excellent decisions and poor ones.

Formal seniority, experience, confidence, or legal authority does not guarantee that a decision is well calibrated or a strategy will succeed. Businesses can fail because of market change, financing, competition, execution, governance, technical failure, and poor decisions by people with substantial authority.

This does not establish that AI judgement is superior in general. It removes a false baseline:

> **The comparison is not fallible AI versus infallible human judgement. It is between alternative decision processes with different information, incentives, costs, strengths, and failure modes.**

For a defined task, an AI system may be better, worse, or complementary to a human decision-maker. That is an empirical question.

## 6. Separate analysis, judgement, authority, and accountability

| Concept | Question | Can AI participate? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Analysis** | What does the evidence indicate? | Yes. Models can retrieve, calculate, compare, summarise, and reason over supplied information. |
| **Judgement / evaluation** | Which option appears better under stated goals, evidence, and trade-offs? | Yes, subject to task-specific reliability and limits. |
| **Authority** | Who or what may commit money, make an offer, change a system, or bind the organisation? | Sometimes delegable within defined bounds; legal and organisational rules set the limit. |
| **Accountability / recourse** | Who must answer for the outcome, correct failures, and provide legal or contractual recourse? | Organisational and legal design determines this; model capability alone does not. |

This is why **“a human made the decision”** and **“a human is legally accountable for the decision”** are different statements.

It is also why an AI system can perform substantial evaluative work without becoming a legal person or company officer.

## 7. Human-in-the-loop is a control pattern, not a quality guarantee

Human review can add independent challenge, domain context, authority that cannot be delegated, relationship value, exception handling, or legally required approval.

But adding a person does not automatically make the workflow correct. A reviewer may be rushed, under-qualified, overconfident, inattentive, or reduced to rubber-stamping machine output. Conversely, automated checks can be more consistent than manual review for well-defined conditions.

Assurance should match the failure mode. It may include:

- deterministic tests;
- retrieval and source verification;
- cross-model or multi-method checks;
- statistical monitoring;
- specialist evaluation;
- human approval where law, authority, contract, or relationship requires it;
- escalation of uncertain cases;
- combinations of the above.

> **Human-in-the-loop is an architecture choice. It is not a synonym for quality.**

## 8. Evaluate alternative actors by the same outcome where possible

Useful comparison dimensions include:

- correctness or acceptance rate;
- calibration and uncertainty handling;
- consistency;
- speed and availability;
- cost per acceptable outcome;
- customer satisfaction;
- conversion or resolution rate where appropriate;
- evidence use;
- adaptability to new context;
- compliance and safety;
- recovery from mistakes;
- escalation behaviour;
- auditability;
- resilience when tools, models, or people are unavailable.

Some dimensions cannot be collapsed into a score. Legal authority, fiduciary duty, employment responsibility, and personal relationships are not just benchmark metrics.

The point is symmetry: **do not assume the human wins before measuring the task, and do not assume the AI wins because it is newer or faster.**

## 9. Embodiment changes presence, not the underlying questions

An embodied AI system can combine:

**sensors + perception + realtime conversation + planning + tools + physical actuation + safety controls + identity and permissions**

Such a system could perform some customer-facing or presentation functions that once required physical human presence.

Whether it is a *good* salesperson, host, guide, presenter, service representative, or public speaker still depends on evidence.

Embodiment may add physical presence, mobility, demonstrations, on-site sensing, continuous availability, consistent delivery, multilingual interaction, and integration with digital systems. It may also add hardware reliability, physical safety, maintenance, privacy, social acceptance, accessibility, security, and recovery risks.

The right comparison is **complete system versus complete system for a particular interaction and risk level**, not robot versus human in the abstract.

## 10. Apply the same capability test across the organisation

AI can assist or automate parts of research, analysis, forecasting, reporting, presentation, customer communication, proposal development, planning, option comparison, monitoring, negotiation support, decision preparation, and coordination.

Therefore technical staff should not be required to prove their continuing value while management judgement is treated as inherently non-automatable.

Ask across roles and systems:

> **What outcome does this function improve? Which parts can be performed by agents? Which authority or accountability remains? What evidence shows the resulting human, AI, automated, or hybrid system is better?**

## Authority-and-assurance map

For any AI-enabled business interaction or decision, produce this short map.

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| **Outcome** | What result the workflow is meant to improve. |
| **Primary actor** | Human, model/agent, deterministic automation, or hybrid. |
| **Evaluation role** | Who or what analyses evidence and compares options. |
| **Authority boundary** | What the actor may recommend, communicate, spend, change, approve, or commit. |
| **Assurance mechanism** | Tests, retrieval/provenance, automated evaluator, second model, specialist check, human approval, monitoring, or a combination. |
| **Escalation trigger** | Uncertainty, policy boundary, high consequence, customer request, abnormal condition, failed check, or other trigger. |
| **Escalation destination** | Stronger model, specialist, manager, deterministic recovery path, another system, or human approver. |
| **Audit evidence** | What logs, source records, calculations, decisions, or tool traces are retained. |
| **Accountability / recourse** | Which legal/organisational party must answer for failures and provide correction. |
| **Success measure** | Accuracy, resolution, conversion, time, cost, satisfaction, risk, or another outcome measure. |

Example decision statement:

> **[Actor/system] performs [task] within [authority boundary]. We use [assurance] because the main failure mode is [risk]. Cases meeting [trigger] escalate to [destination]. The organisation remains accountable through [recourse]. We will judge the design by [outcome measure].**

This is the practical output of the article: it separates **who can do the thinking** from **who may act** and **who must answer for the result**.

## 11. The principle for decision-makers

The compact version is:

> **Do not confuse human involvement with judgement, judgement with authority, or authority with accountability.**

And the positive version is:

> **Use the actor—human, AI, automated system, or hybrid—that produces the best evidenced outcome within the required authority, accountability, safety, and economic constraints.**

This principle is deliberately symmetrical. It does not assume people are obsolete, and it does not assume AI remains a junior assistant forever.
