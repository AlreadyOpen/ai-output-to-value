Open, evidence-led guidance for business decisions

From AI output to business value.

A free or inexpensive AI agent can generate remarkably complete work—and increasingly analyse, communicate, plan, and interact with customers. This guide helps separate what has actually been demonstrated from what still needs to be established before a client, operational, or commercial claim is made.

Use AI ambitiously. Keep the claims clear.

Four distinctions worth remembering

Access is not capability. A licence—or a free agent—provides access to capability, not proof that the organisation can use it effectively for a particular job.
Output is not completion. Generation may be only one part of specification, evaluation, integration, delivery, and support.
Judgement is not authority. A human or AI may evaluate options; permission to act and accountability for the outcome are separate questions.
Activity is not value. More prompts, tokens, generated code, licences, meetings, or travel are not outcomes by themselves.

Decision first

Use the ladder as a stop rule, not a score.

Do not ask how many boxes a project has completed. Ask what decision is being made, then require the weakest claim that is sufficient for that decision.

Typical decision threshold — adjust for consequence, reversibility, regulation and contract.
Decision soughtClaim that normally matters
Keep exploring?Output — enough to reproduce the artefact/action and learn from it.
May someone rely on it?Deliverable — fit for the named use against explicit acceptance criteria.
May we sell, operate or support it repeatedly?Capability — owners, controls, fallback and operating process exist.
Did it change the result?Outcome — the named measure moved versus a baseline.
Should we scale, renew or stop?Value — the outcome is worth full relevant cost, risk and alternatives.
Do not average the six claims.

Strong Access and Output do not make a project partly Deliverable. A missing decision-critical claim cannot be cancelled out by strength somewhere else.

This project's decision framework

Six different claims a business may need to establish.

The arrows are a memory aid, not six mandatory steps. A disposable prototype can create a valuable learning outcome without ever becoming an operational service.

  1. 01

    Access

    We have a model, API, subscription, agent, or tool.

    Does not prove effective use.
  2. 02

    Output

    The system produced an artefact or performed an action.

    Does not prove correctness or client fit.
  3. 03

    Deliverable

    The result is fit for a defined intended use.

    Does not prove repeatability.
  4. 04

    Capability

    The organisation can verify, operate, support, maintain, and improve it.

    Does not prove a valuable outcome.
  5. 05

    Outcome

    Something meaningful changed.

    Can include learning and uncertainty removed.
  6. 06

    Value

    The outcome is worth the full cost, risk, and trade-offs.

    Does not imply every task needs this claim.

Reverse planning: What outcome do we need, and what evidence, capability and work would make that outcome plausible?

The substance gap

A capable agent is not automatically a capable supplier.

A simple example: a contact form can look complete while enquiries never reach the business. The interface exists; the promised workflow does not.

Capability 1

Tool capability

Can the agent generate the website, application, report, workflow, integration, deployment, analysis, or customer response?

Capability 2

Job substance

Does the result reflect the actual requirements, business rules, environment, integrations, constraints, evidence, and relevant risks?

Capability 3

Delivery capability

Can the supplier verify, explain, deploy, support, maintain, correct, and stand behind the result?

Do not mix the terms

They describe different dimensions, and they can overlap.

Way of working

Vibe coding

Prompt-driven software creation and rapid iteration, sometimes with limited inspection of the generated implementation.

AI involvement

AI-assisted work

AI contributed to producing or performing the work. The term alone says nothing about review, controls, or quality.

Practice

Responsible AI-assisted practice

The workflow includes proportionate evaluation, ownership, and controls for the intended use.

Quality problem

AI slop / workslop

Output that appears finished but lacks enough substance, correctness, context, or usefulness for the person who must use it.

A vibe-coded artefact is AI-assisted. Whether it is useful, responsible for the intended use, or low-value output is a separate assessment.

AI can do business work too

Do not reserve “judgement” as a human-only category.

AI systems can analyse evidence, compare alternatives, plan, recommend, prepare proposals, interact with customers, adapt explanations, and make bounded decisions. Humans can make excellent decisions and poor ones too. The useful question is which process performs well enough for this task.

Capability

Judgement / evaluation

Which option appears better under the available evidence, goals, and trade-offs?

Can be performed by humans, AI systems, automated methods, or hybrids—and should be evaluated rather than assumed.

Permission

Authority

Who or what is permitted to commit money, make an offer, change a system, or bind the organisation?

Capability does not automatically confer authority.

Responsibility

Accountability / recourse

Where does responsibility for the outcome sit, and who must correct failures or provide recourse?

A human approval click is not automatically meaningful oversight.

Human-in-the-loop is a control pattern, not a quality guarantee. Use automated checks, models, specialists, people, or hybrid assurance according to the failure mode and consequence.

Human / AI / hybrid interfaces

Same standard does not mean the same interface.

A human can use visible controls. An agent can use a structured tool surface. A hybrid workflow can use both. Actor-neutral evaluation means applying the same intended-use, authority, evidence, failure-handling and outcome standards—not forcing every actor through the same user interface.

Human channel

Visible UI

Forms, menus, direct manipulation and conversation can remain the best interface for a person.

Agent channel

WebMCP structured tools

WebMCP lets a page expose structured application actions through document.modelContext so compatible agents can discover and invoke them instead of guessing every action from the visual UI.

Hybrid control

Different interface, same claim test

Agent actions can still require confirmation, policy checks, audit evidence, fallback or escalation when consequence or authority demands it.

WebMCP changes the channel, not the claim.

Structured agent access is useful Access/Output capability. It does not by itself establish Deliverable, organisational Capability, Outcome or Value.

Option value

Learning can be the outcome.

Cheap, fast prototyping can be valuable even when nothing reaches production. A two-day experiment that kills a weak idea, exposes a difficult integration, or changes the customer conversation can create value by removing uncertainty before a larger commitment.

Outcome

Decision made

The experiment changed what the organisation will do next.

Measure

Uncertainty removed

Record time to decision, assumptions tested, experiment cost and larger spend avoided.

Discipline

Stop when you know enough

Do not force every useful prototype through production-readiness work that would not change the decision.

Use this in a meeting

Eight questions before the next decision.

  1. 1

    What exactly have we demonstrated?
    Separate a working demo from assumptions about readiness or demand.

  2. 2

    What did the AI know, and what did it infer?
    Make generated assumptions visible.

  3. 3

    What remains before the intended use?
    Name unresolved work and decisions.

  4. 4

    Which work disappeared, and which work moved elsewhere?
    Look beyond generation time.

  5. 5

    Which actor or combination performs the task or decision best?
    Compare human, AI, automated, and hybrid processes rather than assuming the answer.

  6. 6

    Where do authority, accountability, verification, approval, operation, and support sit?
    Make decision rights and recourse explicit.

  7. 7

    Which business outcome are we trying to change?
    Include learning or uncertainty removed when that is the purpose.

  8. 8

    What evidence would justify the next decision?
    Decide what would change the answer—and when to stop.

 Industry research · registered claim pending independent review DORA — AI as an amplifier

DORA's 2025 research characterises AI primarily as an amplifier of an organisation's existing strengths and weaknesses; the surrounding system matters to the return.

 Research study · registered claim pending independent review Generative AI at Work

In the registered NBER claim, 5,179 support agents are studied and access to the assistant is associated with a 14% average increase in issues resolved per hour, with heterogeneous effects across workers.

 Official guidance UK Government — AI Playbook

Lifecycle governance, expertise, meaningful human control, testing, monitoring, and accountability.

Vendor examples remain useful capability evidence, but they are not the foundation of the project's central argument.

Beyond the API key — and beyond the free agent

Use AI ambitiously. Establish enough for the decision.

Prototype quickly. Use inexpensive tools when they are enough. Automate technical and business-facing work when the evidence supports it. Then match the claim, evidence, assurance, authority, accountability and economics to the actual decision—without adding process that would not change the answer.
